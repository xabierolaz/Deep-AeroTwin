\abstract{Remote UAV operation requires the pilot to maintain spatial awareness of aircraft state, terrain, obstacles, and mission-relevant objects when direct visual contact or continuous high-quality video may be unavailable. This manuscript presents Pipeline B, a virtual-reality digital-twin operator display in which a pilot wearing a head-mounted display inspects a dynamically reconstructed 3D representation of the operational scene. The implemented runtime path converts in-flight object detections into georeferenced semantic telemetry and uses those data to spawn, update, mark stale, and remove dynamic actors inside an Unreal Engine and Cesium-based environment. The contribution is framed as a human-in-the-loop auxiliary operator display, not as autonomous flight control, certified detect-and-avoid, or a replacement for regulatory safety channels. The paper defines the runtime contract, entity lifecycle, georeferencing assumptions, VR interaction requirements, safety envelope, claim-to-evidence map, and validation protocol required for a complete VRIH submission. Quantitative results are retained as explicit placeholders until validation experiments, VR demonstration material, geospatial ground truth, network-degradation trials, and human-operator studies are completed.}

\section{Draft Status, Journal Target, and Placeholder Convention}

This manuscript is a controlled working draft for \emph{Virtual Reality \& Intelligent Hardware} (VRIH). The intended article type is a full research paper, not a rapid communication. The reason is evidentiary: the argument requires an architecture, an implemented runtime contract, an immersive display concept, software instrumentation, quantitative results, and a human-operator validation. VRIH rapid communications are limited to four pages, whereas this work needs a complete method and validation package.

This version is not yet a submission-ready experimental article. It is deliberately honest: every unmeasured result, missing figure, unconfirmed hardware detail, statistical test, declaration, or physical-trial outcome is marked with \tbd{identifier and required evidence}. \section{Introduction}

Remote UAV operation is a spatial decision-making problem. A pilot or mission operator must reason about aircraft pose, terrain, hazards, people, vehicles, mission targets, visibility, and communication degradation while often relying on a video stream and a map display. Video provides rich evidence, but it also binds the operator to a camera viewpoint, consumes bandwidth, degrades under compression, and can fail to show objects outside the current field of view. Map displays provide geospatial context, but they usually lack the immediacy and embodied spatial interpretation of a three-dimensional scene. The central problem is therefore not only communication load. It is the pilot's need to maintain situation awareness from partial, delayed, and heterogeneous evidence.

Virtual reality and telepresence research provide the conceptual basis for this operator-display problem. Virtual reality can be understood through the dimensions that determine telepresence, including sensory richness and vividness \cite{steuer1992vr}, while telepresence research distinguishes mediated presence from direct physical presence \cite{sheridan1992telepresence}. In dynamic systems, situation awareness includes perception of relevant elements, comprehension of their meaning, and projection of their future status \cite{endsley1995theory}. Its measurement is nontrivial and must be designed explicitly rather than inferred from subjective impressions alone \cite{endsley1995measurement,moens2026meta}. Workload and human-factor measures such as NASA-TLX, SAGAT, SART, and simulator-sickness questionnaires are therefore part of the evidence burden for a VR operator display \cite{hart1988tlx,hart2006tlx,endsley1998sagat_sart,kennedy1993ssq}.

Pipeline B addresses this problem by converting detected objects into a persistent, spatially organized digital-twin layer. A detection source sends object-class, confidence, identity, timestamp, and estimated position data to a ground-station service called Brain. Brain normalizes and publishes the live entity layer, and Unreal Engine renders the resulting actors over Cesium geospatial context for an operator wearing a head-mounted display. The reconstructed scene is not presented as ground truth. It is an auxiliary operator display in which every dynamic actor must carry freshness, confidence, source, and uncertainty semantics.

The main research question is:

\begin{quote}
Can a VR-equipped UAV operator maintain better spatial awareness of mission-relevant objects under constrained or degraded communication than with video-only, map-only, or non-immersive hybrid displays, while preserving explicit visibility of stale, uncertain, or missing information?
\end{quote}

This question cannot be answered by architecture alone. It requires a system demonstration, bandwidth measurements, latency measurements, geospatial error validation, degradation tests, and a human-operator study. This paper therefore separates what has already been implemented or specified from the physical and human evidence still required.

The intended contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item a VR digital-twin operator-display architecture for dynamic UAV scene reconstruction from in-flight detections;
    \item a concrete runtime contract linking detection ingestion, Brain state publication, Unreal/Cesium actor lifecycle, and HMD visualization;
    \item a formalized entity model, georeferencing model, latency model, bandwidth model, stale-state policy, and uncertainty-display requirement;
    \item a claim-to-evidence map that prevents architecture claims from being confused with validated experimental results;
    \item a VRIH-oriented validation protocol covering bandwidth, latency, geospatial accuracy, packet loss, tracking stability, human utility, low-visibility limits, safety envelope, and reproducibility.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work and Positioning}

Pipeline B sits at the intersection of immersive teleoperation, digital twins, semantic/task-oriented communication, UAV perception, UAV geolocation, and degraded-link human-computer interaction. Its novelty is not the invention of any one of those areas. The novelty claim must be narrower: an implemented and measurable runtime path for reconstructing UAV mission-relevant entities inside a VR digital twin from in-flight detections.

\subsection{Immersive Teleoperation and Situation Awareness}

Immersive interfaces have been studied as a way to improve operator perception in unmanned-vehicle control. Immersive telepresence with mixed reality has been evaluated for unmanned vehicle operation and situation awareness \cite{fabris2021immersive}. Multi-robot interface studies show that immersion and predictive display elements can affect operator situation awareness \cite{roldan2017multirobot}. Recent work on VR teleoperation has also explored visual augmentation of live streams for unmanned vehicles \cite{luo2024visual} and compared 3D visualization and VR display designs for remote monitoring tasks \cite{buchner2025remote}. These studies support the premise that immersion may change operator awareness, but they also show that the effect must be measured rather than assumed.

UAV-specific VR and digital-twin studies are directly relevant. Augmented-reality mobile digital twins have been proposed for UAV wildfire-prevention workflows \cite{costa2025ardigitaltwin}. VR-based teleoperation has been applied to UAV-manipulator systems \cite{yang2025uavmanipulator}. Recent VR digital-twin work on UAV control studies how different points of view affect user experience and performance \cite{vona2025uavdt}. Pipeline B differs from these works by focusing on dynamic reconstruction from semantic detections rather than only viewpoint control, simulated piloting, or manipulation interfaces.

\subsection{Digital Twins for VR and UAV Systems}

Digital twin research has established terminology, design implications, and system taxonomies. VRIH has explicitly highlighted the connection between virtual reality, intelligent hardware, and digital twins \cite{lv2022vrihdt}; VRIH has also published digital-twin work in applied AI contexts \cite{ahmed2022vrihdt}. Broader surveys characterize digital twins as cyber-physical representations requiring data synchronization, modeling, and operational purpose \cite{jones2020dt,barricelli2019dt,singh2021dt}. For UAVs, digital twins have been discussed in beyond-5G contexts \cite{lv2021uavdt} and UAV-swarm cooperation \cite{lei2021uavswarms}. These works motivate digital twins as data-driven operational interfaces, but they do not by themselves validate a specific UAV-to-HMD operator workflow.

\subsection{Semantic and Task-Oriented Communication}

Semantic communication and task-oriented communication shift the objective from reconstructing every bit of a source signal to transmitting information useful for a downstream task. Surveys and early systems describe semantic communication as a future wireless-network paradigm \cite{luo2022semantic}, while deep-learning semantic communication systems demonstrate end-to-end learned communication mechanisms \cite{xie2021semantic}. Task-oriented edge inference uses information bottleneck ideas to trade communication cost against task performance \cite{shao2022edgeinference}. Edge-video analytics extends the idea to video-derived features rather than full video reconstruction \cite{shao2024edgevideo}, and cooperative edge inference studies multi-device task-oriented communication \cite{shao2023multidevice}.

Pipeline B should not claim to solve semantic communication theory. Its object stream is closer to engineered semantic telemetry: it sends detected entities and their display-relevant attributes instead of a full video stream. The correct claim is operational and measurable: for the selected operator task, entity telemetry may reduce bandwidth while preserving relevant spatial information. That claim remains \tbd{TBD-BW bandwidth evidence and task-completeness evidence}.

\subsection{UAV Detection, Geolocation, and Communication Constraints}

UAV object detection is mature enough that this paper should not frame detector choice as its primary novelty. Real-time object detection based on UAV remote sensing has been extensively reviewed, including hardware, latency, energy, and onboard processing concerns \cite{cao2023uavdetection}. Pipeline B can use YOLO or another detector as a source, but the contribution begins after detection: entity encoding, georeferencing assumptions, live actor management, stale-state display, and operator use.

Vision-based UAV target geolocation has a long literature. Fixed-wing miniature air vehicle work localized ground targets from image pixels, pose, and camera geometry \cite{barber2006geolocation}. UAV video imagery studies have combined target geolocation, tracking, and filtering \cite{hosseinpoor2016geolocation}. Vision-based tracking and motion estimation for moving targets has been demonstrated with UAV video and telemetry \cite{dobrokhodov2008tracking}. More recent work improves target geolocation using multi-view aerial images in long-range oblique photography \cite{liu2024multiview}. These references make a strict point: Pipeline B must say ``estimated georeferencing under calibrated camera and pose assumptions'', not ``triangulation'' or ``ground-truth localization'' unless \tbd{TBD-GEO} is completed.

UAV communication constraints are also well established. UAV wireless communications bring opportunities and link-design challenges \cite{zeng2016uavcomm}. Surveys identify connectivity, reliability, routing, security, and quality-of-service issues in UAV communication networks \cite{gupta2016uavcomm,hayat2016uavnetworks}. Multi-tier drone architectures for 5G/B5G networks further underline the role of aerial platforms in constrained and heterogeneous communication systems \cite{sekander2018multitier}. Pipeline B therefore cannot assume that video, semantic telemetry, command-and-control, and pose channels degrade identically. Robustness must be tested under explicit packet loss, jitter, interruption, and stale-state conditions.

\subsection{Simulation, Runtime Engines, and Software Infrastructure}

Unreal-based vehicle simulation and MAVLink-oriented tooling are not novel by themselves. AirSim provides high-fidelity visual and physical simulation for autonomous vehicles \cite{shah2017airsim}, and DroneVR demonstrates a web virtual-reality simulator for drone operators \cite{nguyen2019dronevr}. Pipeline B uses Unreal Engine and Cesium as rendering and geospatial infrastructure. Those components are implementation choices. Their use is relevant to reproducibility and HMD performance, but the paper's scientific argument depends on whether dynamic semantic telemetry produces a useful, measurable operator display.

\subsection{Positioning Statement}

The paper's novelty should be stated conservatively:

\begin{quote}
Pipeline B integrates detection-derived semantic telemetry, estimated georeferencing, Brain-mediated runtime state, Unreal/Cesium dynamic actor reconstruction, and HMD-facing VR interaction into a single evaluable UAV operator-display workflow.
\end{quote}

The paper should avoid claiming that it substitutes for video, proves information-theoretic semantic optimality, guarantees safety for piloting, performs certified detect-and-avoid, or solves BVLOS regulation. Those claims are outside the available evidence and outside the proper scope of the architecture.

\section{System Overview}

\subsection{Operator Scenario}

The target scenario is a UAV mission in which a remote operator needs to monitor terrain, aircraft state, obstacles, people, vehicles, or mission objects while communication quality is constrained or intermittently degraded. The operator wears an HMD and can inspect a synthetic 3D scene containing terrain context, UAV pose, detected entities, freshness indicators, and uncertainty indicators.

The proposed display is auxiliary. It is not the primary command-and-control safety channel. It should be used alongside regulatory control links, video when available, map displays, flight telemetry, and operational procedures. The display is valuable only if it helps the operator understand spatial relationships and object persistence without hiding uncertainty.

\subsection{Architecture}

Pipeline B has five runtime layers:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Detection layer}: a YOLO or other detection source observes image frames or sensor data and emits object detections.
    \item \textbf{Georeferencing layer}: detections are associated with estimated world coordinates using camera, pose, attitude, and terrain assumptions.
    \item \textbf{Brain layer}: the ground-station service ingests detections, normalizes entities, manages freshness, and publishes operator-display state.
    \item \textbf{Rendering layer}: Unreal Engine, Cesium terrain, and a telemetry component reconstruct dynamic actors from the current entity layer.
    \item \textbf{Operator layer}: the HMD presents an immersive scene with entity class, confidence, age, uncertainty, and stale-state encodings.
\end{enumerate}

The current implemented path is organized around two HTTP interfaces:

\begin{enumerate}

    \item Brain normalizes source identity, class, confidence, timestamp, and position;
    \item Brain exposes the current operator-display state through \code{GET /api/ui/data};
    \item \code{UPorceTelemetryComponent} in Unreal polls the state endpoint;
    \item Unreal spawns, updates, marks stale, or despawns actors using \code{entity\_id} or \code{object\_id};

\subsection{Entity Contract}

Each reconstructed object is represented as a display entity:

\begin{equation}
e_i(t) = \{ id_i, c_i, q_i, p_i, \Sigma_i, s_i, a_i, g_i, m_i \},
\label{eq:entity}
\end{equation}

where \(id_i\) is a stable identity, \(c_i\) is the class label, \(q_i\) is detector confidence, \(p_i\) is estimated position, \(\Sigma_i\) is position uncertainty, \(s_i\) is source identifier, \(a_i\) is age or freshness, \(g_i\) is optional geometry, and \(m_i\) is metadata for display and logging.

The minimum runtime fields are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \code{entity\_id} or \code{object\_id};
    \item object class and optional subclass;
    \item confidence score and confidence-threshold policy;
    \item source frame timestamp and Brain receive timestamp;
    \item geospatial position \code{lat}/\code{lon}/\code{alt} when available;
    \item local coordinates \code{world\_m} when available;
    \item approximate footprint, bounding volume, or class-specific geometry;
    \item source identifier and detector metadata;
    \item freshness, stale flag, and uncertainty fields \tbd{TBD-SAFETY freshness and uncertainty schema}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Actor Lifecycle}

The display actor lifecycle is:

\begin{equation}
\text{new} \rightarrow \text{confirmed} \rightarrow \text{updated} \rightarrow \text{stale} \rightarrow \text{removed}.
\label{eq:lifecycle}
\end{equation}

An entity is marked stale when:

\begin{equation}
a_i(t) = t - t_i > \tau_{\mathrm{stale}},
\label{eq:stale}
\end{equation}

where \(t_i\) is the last valid update time and \(\tau_{\mathrm{stale}}\) is the maximum age allowed before the display visually demotes the actor. The threshold must be selected from the operator task and verified experimentally \tbd{TBD-SAFETY max stale age}. An actor may be removed when:

\begin{equation}
a_i(t) > \tau_{\mathrm{remove}},
\label{eq:remove}
\end{equation}

with \(\tau_{\mathrm{remove}} > \tau_{\mathrm{stale}}\), unless the system policy requires persistent ghost actors for recent hazards \tbd{TBD-SAFETY despawn policy}.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Detection-to-World Projection}

The georeferencing step must be framed as estimated world-coordinate assignment under calibrated camera and pose assumptions. For a pixel observation \(z=[u,v]^T\), the normalized camera ray is:

\begin{equation}
\tilde{r}_c = K^{-1}
\begin{bmatrix}
u \\
v \\
1
\end{bmatrix},
\quad
r_c = \frac{\tilde{r}_c}{\|\tilde{r}_c\|},
\label{eq:camera-ray}
\end{equation}

where \(K\) is the camera intrinsic matrix. The corresponding world-frame ray is:

\begin{equation}
r_w = R_{wb} R_{bc} r_c,
\label{eq:world-ray}
\end{equation}

where \(R_{bc}\) maps camera coordinates into the UAV body frame and \(R_{wb}\) maps the body frame into the world frame. Given UAV position \(p_u\), an estimated object position is:

\begin{equation}
\hat{p}_o = p_u + \lambda r_w.
\label{eq:object-position}
\end{equation}

For a local planar ground assumption \(z=z_g\), \(\lambda\) is:

\begin{equation}
\lambda = \frac{z_g - p_{u,z}}{r_{w,z}}.
\label{eq:lambda-plane}
\end{equation}

For terrain intersection, \(z_g\) must be replaced by a terrain or map model \tbd{TBD-GEO terrain intersection model}. This projection is sensitive to camera calibration, UAV attitude, timestamp synchronization, terrain height, lens distortion, and the chosen pixel point for the detection. Therefore, the projected object position is an estimate, not a proof of target location.

\subsection{Uncertainty Propagation}

The display must avoid overconfidence. The uncertainty of the estimated object position can be approximated by first-order propagation:

\begin{equation}
\Sigma_p \approx J_x \Sigma_x J_x^T,
\label{eq:uncertainty}
\end{equation}

where \(x\) contains pixel coordinates, intrinsics, extrinsics, UAV pose, attitude, and terrain parameters, \(\Sigma_x\) is their covariance, and \(J_x\) is the Jacobian of the projection model with respect to those variables. The current draft does not yet have calibrated covariance values \tbd{TBD-GEO covariance inputs}. At minimum, the display must show a conservative uncertainty radius or confidence band \tbd{TBD-SAFETY uncertainty visualization}.

\subsection{Semantic Telemetry Bandwidth}

For a mission interval of duration \(T\), with \(N\) entity messages of serialized size \(|m_k|\) bytes, semantic telemetry bitrate is:

\begin{equation}
B_{\mathrm{sem}} = \frac{8}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{N} |m_k|.
\label{eq:bandwidth}
\end{equation}

Packet rate is:

\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{pkt}} = \frac{N}{T}.
\label{eq:packet-rate}
\end{equation}

The corresponding video baseline must be measured over the same mission duration, scene, and link profile:

\begin{equation}
\Delta B = \frac{B_{\mathrm{video}} - B_{\mathrm{sem}}}{B_{\mathrm{video}}}.
\label{eq:bandwidth-reduction}
\end{equation}

A bandwidth reduction is only meaningful if the semantic display preserves the entities needed for the operator task \tbd{TBD-BW entity completeness}.

\subsection{Latency Decomposition}

End-to-end latency from source event to visible HMD update is:

\begin{equation}
L_{\mathrm{e2e}} = t_{\mathrm{hmd}} - t_{\mathrm{det}},
\label{eq:e2e-latency}
\end{equation}

and should be decomposed as:

\begin{equation}
L_{\mathrm{e2e}} =
L_{\mathrm{det}} +
L_{\mathrm{uplink}} +
L_{\mathrm{brain}} +
L_{\mathrm{poll}} +
L_{\mathrm{actor}} +
L_{\mathrm{render}} +
L_{\mathrm{hmd}}.
\label{eq:latency-components}
\end{equation}

The software-only segments \(L_{\mathrm{brain}}\), \(L_{\mathrm{poll}}\), and \(L_{\mathrm{actor}}\) can be instrumented immediately. The complete source-to-HMD measurement requires an HMD and a real or instrumented detection source \tbd{TBD-LAT e2e measurement}.

\subsection{VR Operator Display Requirements}

The VRIH submission must describe the display as an immersive human-computer interface, not as a generic 3D plot. The paper therefore needs the following details:

\begin{itemize}
    \item HMD model, runtime, refresh rate, resolution, tracking mode, and seated/standing configuration \tbd{TBD-DEMO HMD configuration};
    \item viewpoint modes: UAV-centric, external chase, top-down inspection, operator-selected orbit, or fixed mission overview \tbd{TBD-DEMO viewpoint modes};
    \item visual encodings for class, confidence, age, source, stale state, and uncertainty \tbd{TBD-SAFETY display encoding};
    \item interaction primitives: inspect entity, filter class, freeze history, recenter view, zoom, switch viewpoint, show raw source, and display uncertainty \tbd{TBD-HUMAN interaction model};
    \item fallback state when detections, pose, map, or network updates are stale \tbd{TBD-SAFETY fallback rule};
    \item comfort safeguards: frame rate, locomotion mode, camera motion, cybersickness screening, and session duration \tbd{TBD-HUMAN comfort protocol}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Evaluation Protocol}

\subsection{Research Questions and Hypotheses}

The evaluation should answer the following research questions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1}: Does semantic entity telemetry reduce bandwidth compared with video baselines for the same mission interval?
    \item \textbf{RQ2}: Is the source-to-HMD latency low enough for an auxiliary operator display?
    \item \textbf{RQ3}: Are estimated actor positions accurate enough, and is displayed uncertainty calibrated?
    \item \textbf{RQ4}: Does the display expose loss, jitter, interruption, stale detections, and tracking fragmentation without false freshness?
    \item \textbf{RQ5}: Does the VR display improve operator spatial awareness or task success relative to video-only, map-only, or non-immersive hybrid baselines?
    \item \textbf{RQ6}: Do low-visibility conditions change the system from useful to misleading when the sensor layer fails?
\end{itemize}

The corresponding hypotheses are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{H1}: semantic telemetry has lower mean and p95 bandwidth than video baselines while preserving required entity state \tbd{TBD-BW};
    \item \textbf{H2}: actor updates become visible in the HMD below a declared auxiliary-display latency threshold \tbd{TBD-LAT threshold};
    \item \textbf{H3}: geospatial error remains inside the displayed uncertainty envelope \tbd{TBD-GEO};
    \item \textbf{H4}: stale or uncertain actors are not perceived as fresh truth under link degradation \tbd{TBD-LOSS and TBD-HUMAN overtrust};
    \item \textbf{H5}: VR improves spatial awareness or task success without unacceptable workload or cybersickness \tbd{TBD-HUMAN};
    \item \textbf{H6}: any low-visibility benefit is conditional on validated sensor detections \tbd{TBD-LOWVIS}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Baselines}

The minimum baselines are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Video-only}: compressed camera feed with flight telemetry but no semantic reconstruction.
    \item \textbf{Map-only}: map or Cesium terrain with UAV pose but no dynamic semantic actors.
    \item \textbf{Non-immersive hybrid}: desktop or monitor view with map/video plus semantic overlays.
    \item \textbf{VR digital twin}: HMD display with terrain, UAV pose, dynamic actors, freshness, confidence, and uncertainty.
\end{itemize}

The final experiment must specify whether all baselines receive the same underlying detections, video source, network profile, and task instructions \tbd{TBD-HUMAN baseline parity}.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis Plan}

The statistical plan must be written before collecting human-subject results. At minimum, the paper should report:

\begin{itemize}
    \item sample size rationale and power or precision target \tbd{TBD-STAT sample size};
    \item within-subject or between-subject design, counterbalancing, and randomization \tbd{TBD-STAT design};
    \item primary endpoint, likely task success or spatial-awareness score \tbd{TBD-STAT primary endpoint};
    \item secondary endpoints: workload, near misses, overtrust, completion time, cybersickness \tbd{TBD-STAT secondary endpoints};
    \item model family, e.g., repeated-measures ANOVA, linear mixed model, generalized mixed model, or nonparametric alternative \tbd{TBD-STAT model};
    \item effect sizes and confidence intervals \tbd{TBD-STAT effect sizes};
    \item correction for multiple comparisons where needed \tbd{TBD-STAT multiplicity};
    \item exclusion criteria for simulator sickness, tracking failure, or incomplete trials \tbd{TBD-STAT exclusions}.
\end{itemize}

This plan is intentionally included before results to prevent post-hoc metric selection.

\section{Limitations and Safety Envelope}

Pipeline B can mislead an operator if uncertainty is hidden. The following limitations are part of the system definition:

\begin{itemize}
    \item false negatives are critical because an absent actor may mean an undetected object, not a safe empty space;
    \item stale actors can look authoritative unless age, source, and confidence are visible;
    \item a visually stable synthetic scene can increase overtrust;
    \item object metadata, timestamps, and geolocation can leak privacy-sensitive information;
    \item low visibility, glare, occlusion, or smoke reduce sensor performance unless alternative sensors are validated;
    \item Cesium, map, terrain, geoid, origin, or coordinate-system errors can become actor-placement errors;
    \item timestamp drift can turn correct detections into incorrect moving-object states;
    \item VR can introduce cybersickness, attentional tunneling, fatigue, and display occlusion;
    \item BVLOS use requires detect-and-avoid, command-and-control reliability, operational design-domain limits, and regulatory compliance beyond this display.
\end{itemize}

The safety envelope must define:

\begin{itemize}
    \item maximum stale-object age \tbd{TBD-SAFETY max stale age};
    \item maximum position uncertainty radius \tbd{TBD-SAFETY max uncertainty radius};
    \item minimum detection confidence for confirmed actor display \tbd{TBD-SAFETY minimum confidence};
    \item fallback rule when telemetry, detections, pose, or map context become stale \tbd{TBD-SAFETY fallback rule};
    \item allowed operating envelope by altitude, range, speed, terrain, lighting, link type, and sensor configuration \tbd{TBD-SAFETY allowed operating envelope};
    \item explicit prohibition against using the display as certified detect-and-avoid unless separate certification evidence exists \tbd{TBD-SAFETY regulatory wording}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}

The central scientific risk is overclaiming. A reviewer can accept a paper that defines an honest VR operator-display architecture and validates it carefully. A reviewer should reject a paper that implies safe piloting, primary navigation, or BVLOS compliance from a visual prototype. Pipeline B is therefore framed as an auxiliary display for spatial awareness under degraded or constrained communication.

The second risk is treating a beautiful HMD scene as evidence. The scene only becomes evidence when it is tied to logs, timestamps, ground truth, and user-task outcomes. This is why the manuscript includes the claim-to-evidence map and placeholder result tables. Those tables are not decoration; they are the minimum conditions for the final paper.

The third risk is confusing semantic telemetry with semantic communication theory. Pipeline B transmits task-relevant entity state, but it does not currently prove semantic optimality, mutual information bounds, or learned communication objectives. The paper should therefore use ``semantic telemetry'' in an engineering sense and reserve stronger theoretical language for future work.

The fourth risk is human factors. VR may improve spatial awareness, but it may also increase workload, sickness, distraction, or overtrust. A VRIH submission must therefore include a real HMD task, baselines, subjective and objective measures, and a statistical plan.

\section{Conclusion}

Pipeline B is best framed for VRIH as a human-in-the-loop VR digital-twin operator display for dynamic UAV scene reconstruction from in-flight detections. The implemented runtime path converts detected objects into semantic telemetry, publishes a live entity layer through Brain, and reconstructs dynamic actors inside an Unreal/Cesium scene for HMD-based operator inspection.

The paper does not yet contain final experimental evidence. It now contains the full structure needed to reach a submission-ready version: state-of-the-art positioning, a conservative novelty claim, runtime contract, formal georeferencing and latency models, bandwidth and stale-state definitions, VR interaction requirements, evaluation protocol, placeholder result tables, safety envelope, statistical plan, and declarations. The remaining blockers are physical and experimental: HMD demonstration, real or instrumented source-to-HMD latency, calibrated geospatial validation, packet-loss trials, dynamic tracking validation, human-operator study, and low-visibility sensor evidence.
